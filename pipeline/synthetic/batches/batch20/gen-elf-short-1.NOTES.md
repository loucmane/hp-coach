## 1. The three binding constraints (RULE 20b), each with the span that discharges it

The brief's own warning, verbatim, because the brief asks for it to be stated:

> *“Three simultaneous binding constraints on one lane slot is the point at which
> a lane brief usually starts producing strained units.”*

And the brief's own resolution, which was applied literally rather than
negotiated: **move the subject far.** Working trawl gear at sea is not a craft
adjacent to a building; it is a different world with its own speakers and its own
surname stock. That single move paid for two of the three constraints outright,
so only one of them actually shaped the topic choice.

### Constraint 1 — quoted voice (deferred twice, not deferrable again)

**DISCHARGED.** A named practitioner speaks in direct quoted speech, and it is a
whole paragraph of the passage, not a fragment:

> “A net is one thing on the deck and another thing down there,” he says. “Tow
> it, and every mesh pulls long and thin. The hole you measured with your hand is
> a slot by then. A haddock is round, and it has to get its width through.”

Quotation marks, a present-tense attribution (“he says”), first-person trade
idiom, and the load-bearing explanation of the whole unit inside the quotation
rather than paraphrased around it. This is *direct* speech, not reported speech:
nothing in the passage says “Hoskadale explains that…”.

Worth recording that this is genuinely new in the lane, not a re-run: **no
shipped ELF one-question short in the bank contains direct speech at all.** All
the quoted-expert machinery in the P5 ELF corpus lives in the long passages and
the cloze units.

Why the subject made it cheap: a quay interview is the *native* register for
this material. Gear knowledge on a small boat is craft knowledge held by the
person towing the net; a piece about it that had no skipper in it would be the
strained version.

### Constraint 2 — leave the domestic building envelope entirely

**DISCHARGED.** The unit contains no wall, roof, chimney, flue, mortar, damp,
brick, tile, joint, cavity, stack, batten, loft or dwelling of any kind. The only
structure named is a trawl cod end. Materials named: netting, twine, mesh. The
setting is a boat, a tow and a harbour.

## 2. RULE 17 (an ordinary name) and its collision with RULE 16 — stated, not fudged

The two rules pull against each other and the unit resolves the tension by
**splitting the two obligations across its two figures**, which is the only way
both can be satisfied at once:

- **RULE 16** governs *coined* names: distance ≥ 2 from every shipped proper noun
  and from real gazetteer/surname neighbours, one-letter variants enumerated by
  hand.
- **RULE 17** requires at least one named figure to carry an *ordinary,
  unremarkable* name, and says one wholly ordinary figure is better than the
  cheap ordinary-given-name-over-coined-surname form. An ordinary name is at
  distance **0** from real name stock by construction; it cannot also satisfy
  RULE 16, and it is not meant to.

So:

- **Trevor Hoskadale** — the coinage, and the figure with invented words in his
  mouth. It therefore carries the *full* RULE 16 burden (§6 below). Ordinary
  given name over a coined surname: the batch18/19 cheap form, applied to the
  figure where a real bearer would do the most damage.
- **Alison Hebden** — the RULE 17 ordinary figure. Both parts are real,
  unremarkable English name stock. It carries the different burden law 16
  actually imposes on a real-stock name: no bearer of the exact full string *in a
  related field*.

The unit contains **exactly one coined surname**, so it cannot present its
coinages as an unbroken cluster — the batch19 complaint was four in one ELF unit
(Calverend / Grendisham / Steadgrove / Ockendale). And the riskier role went to
the coinage: no real-stock name is quoted anywhere in this unit.

## 3. The mechanism, and the sources for it

Verified **before drafting**, because a backwards mechanism is a V-FINAL kill and
the batch15 TYPE-001 short was refuted on exactly that.

**The mechanism in one line:** a diamond mesh elongates along the tow axis under
drag and loses its *lateral* opening, so nominal mesh size does not deliver the
width a round-bodied fish needs; re-hanging the same netting so the meshes stand
as squares holds the opening under load; and the same change does little for
flatfish, whose flattened cross-section already matches a stretched diamond.

| passage claim | source |
|---|---|
| “every mesh pulls long and thin… a slot by then” — diamond mesh closes under tow | Sala, Herrmann, De Carlo, Lucchetti & Brčić, *Effect of Codend Circumference on the Size Selection of Square-Mesh Codends in Trawl Fisheries*, PLOS ONE 11(7) e0160354 (2016): “the drag forces acting on the catch in the aft portion of the codend tend to stretch the meshes in most codend areas, causing a reduction in the opening angle. Only a few rows of diamond meshes… provide an opening angle wide enough for the escapement of round fish species.” Also Depestele, Polet, Van Craeynest & Vandendriessche, *An overview of sea trials with the alternative beam trawl* (ILVO, 2011), citing Stewart & Robinson (1985) and Wileman et al. (1996): “Diamond meshes have the tendency to close when they are stretched… The number of meshes through which fish can escape is thus seriously reduced.” |
| “A haddock is round, and it has to get its width through” | Seafish gear database, *Square Mesh Cod-ends*: “The square shape of the mesh better suits the cross sectional shape of the round fish for escape through the meshes than the shape of flat fish.” |
| two sizes up and nothing changed — the unit's premise | Bak-Jensen, Herrmann & Santos, *The capability of square-meshes and fixed-shape meshes to control codend size selection* (Thünen-Institut): mesh-openness variability “can negate the effect of gear modifications to reduce unwanted catches, such as increasing mesh size. Thereby, it can be questioned how well mesh size works as a regulating tool.” And the turned-mesh comparison: square meshes “maintain their shapes during towing and are hence found to be more selective than diamond mesh codend with same or even larger mesh sizes” (whiting L50 26 cm turned vs 12 cm standard). |
| “the same twine turned right round the bag, so the meshes stood as open squares under the drag” | Bak-Jensen et al., citing Robertson & Stewart (1988): square-mesh codends have “bars that are parallel to both transversal… and the longitudinal direction… the parallel bars should keep the meshes more uniform and open as the tension from the drag forces in the mesh bars does not close the meshes.” |
| “The small haddock went out.” | Seafish, *Square Mesh Cod-ends*: skippers “managed to release in the region of 90% of the small round fish.” |
| “The plaice were much as before, a flat fish and a stretched mesh being much the same shape.” | Depestele et al. (2011), citing Fonteyne & M'Rabet (1992) and Walsh et al. (1992): “square meshes are less selective for flatfish compared to diamond meshes. The rationale behind it was that diamond meshes have a shape similar to the body shape of flatfish, thus allowing an easier passage through the mesh.” *Reviews in Fish Biology and Fisheries* (2025), *Understanding and predicting codend size selection for flatfish species*: flatfish morphology “has often been associated with having good escape properties from elongated diamond meshes in the codend compared to square meshes.” And, at skipper level, Seafish again: “Many skippers have reported that they have seen no difference in the retained catch of flat fish when they have fitted a square mesh cod-end of a similar mesh size to their original diamond mesh.” |
| “He had not bargained for good fish going out too, on the tows when the square bars ran slack.” | Bak-Jensen et al., reporting Krag et al. (2011) and Herrmann et al. (2016): “the tension in the bars in square-mesh is mainly in longitudinal, while the two remaining bars (transversal) may well be slack and deformable. This can result in larger fish being able to escape through square-meshes than desirable.” |

**A distractor rejected on physics, logged because it was rejected on physics.**
An earlier draft used *“a thicker twine… held the meshes apart against the drag”*
as the outside-knowledge distractor. The literature runs the other way — Lowry &
Robertson, *The effect of twine thickness on cod-end selectivity of trawls for
haddock in the North Sea*, Fisheries Research 26 (1996); Kynoch & Zuur (1999);
the PRESEMO modelling study — thicker, stiffer twine *reduces* lateral mesh
opening and lowers L50. The distractor would have been backwards as world fact
rather than merely absent from the text, so it was replaced by C, which is
world-**true** and passage-**false**: the shape TYPE-001 actually wants.

**Not verified:** no ICES working-group report, no EU technical-measures
regulation, no flume-tank study read in full. Mesh sizes are deliberately
unnumbered in the passage (“two mesh sizes”), so the unit asserts no millimetre
figure and no jurisdiction's legal minimum.

## 4. Trap design

Stem: **“What is said about the cod end Hoskadale tows?”** — purely referential.
It names the object and the owner and predicates nothing contested about either.
Corpus-attested frame (*“What is said about the concept of ‘vacuum’?”*, *“What is
said about the 1960s?”*), and deliberately rotated away from *“What are we told
about X?”*, which two of the last four TYPE-001 shorts already used.

| opt | trap (families.md TYPE-001 anatomy) | the span that kills it |
|---|---|---|
| **A** “The two extra mesh sizes did what he expected and let the small haddock out.” | causal link glued onto a descriptive statement + lay outside knowledge (bigger mesh → more escapes). The passage supplies the premise and lets a skim-reader supply the consequence. | “**They did not.**” |
| **B** “The plaice left the bag as readily as the haddock once the meshes were turned.” | true-statement-with-the-actor-swapped. The release *is* real and stated (“The small haddock went out”); B transplants it onto the species the passage explicitly exempts. | “**The plaice were much as before, a flat fish and a stretched mesh being much the same shape.**” |
| **C** “The gain came from a panel of square netting set into the top of the bag.” | plausible outside knowledge sharpened with surface_word_match. A square-mesh panel in the top sheet is a real and standard escape device, so **gear knowledge makes C more attractive, not less**, and the passage's own word “squares” makes it feel anchored. | “**the same twine turned right round the bag**” — the whole cod end re-hung, not an insert. |
| **D** *(key)* “Towing closed the meshes, so the extra size did not give a round fish its width.” | — | the quoted paragraph, read against “They did not. A whole spring on, the boxes at the harbour looked much as they had the year before.” |

Two deliberate balances in the option set:

- **2–2 split by kind.** A and B are outcome claims; C and D are mechanism
  claims. So “pick the one that sounds like a mechanism” does not isolate the
  key.
- **Neither form heuristic works.** `mech.py`'s absolutizer test returns
  **False for all four options** (checked mechanically, not by eye) — so
  strip-the-absolutes selects nothing and M-FORM cannot fire on structure rather
  than on luck. And no option is hedged: no *often*, *tends to*, *mainly*,
  *largely*, *in some cases* anywhere in the four. The key is the flat,
  confident, specific claim, which is the RULE 10 / RULE 13 shape.

Law 11 (no verbatim-true distractor) holds: none of A, B, C is true as written,
so “best fit” is doing no work — each fails against a span a careful reader can
point at.

## 5. Self-blind-solve — arguing FOR each distractor, then killing it on a span

Solved from the passage alone, sceptically, arguing each non-keyed option as if
it were right.

**FOR A.** “He went up two mesh sizes. That is the standard fix and the passage
says he did it. The whole first paragraph is about that change, and the option
just states its purpose — of course a bigger hole lets a small fish out; that is
what mesh regulation *is*.”
**KILLED BY:** *“They did not.”* Three words, sentence two, and the following
sentence measures the non-result over a whole season (“A whole spring on, the
boxes at the harbour looked much as they had the year before”). A is not merely
unsupported — it is the proposition the passage was written to refute. Its
attraction is entirely from outside the text, which is what makes it the strong
skim-trap rather than filler.

**FOR B.** “The passage does say fish went out after the turn, and plaice are
mentioned right there in the same paragraph as the escaping haddock. If squares
release fish, they release fish; nothing in a mesh knows what species it is.”
**KILLED BY:** *“The plaice were much as before, a flat fish and a stretched mesh
being much the same shape.”* This is explicit and it comes with its reason — a
flatfish cross-section already matched the old elongated opening, so the plaice
were never the ones the change was for. B survives only if you read the paragraph
as a list of things that happened rather than as a contrast.

**FOR C.** “The passage is clearly about square meshes; it says the meshes ‘stood
as open squares’. A panel is how square mesh is normally fitted — it is the
standard device. And the option is about *where* the squares are, which the
passage arguably never pins down.”
**KILLED BY:** *“He had the same twine turned right round the bag.”* The passage
does pin it down, twice over: the netting is the **same** twine (not new material
let into it) and it is turned **right round the bag** (the whole circumference,
not one sheet). A panel is an insert; this is a re-hang. C is the option I argued
hardest for, because it is the one that is true about the world and false about
this boat — and that is exactly the `outside_knowledge` trap the family calls
for.

**Verdict: exactly one defensible answer, D.** D is the only claim that survives
its span check, and it is the only one that accounts for *both* halves of the
narrative — why the first change failed and why the second worked.

**Blind floor, stated honestly.** With the stem and options alone and no passage:
nothing marks the key. No absolutizer, no hedge asymmetry, no strict length
outlier, no register split (two mechanism options, two outcome options). For a
typical HP candidate the floor is **1 in 4**. For an adult with trawl-gear
knowledge it is higher and I will not pretend otherwise — they know diamond
meshes close under tow, and D says so. The mitigations are real but partial:
(i) C is *world-true* as a device, so world knowledge alone does not discriminate
between C and D; (ii) D's second clause (“the extra size did not give a round
fish its width”) is this boat's specific outcome, not a textbook fact, and a
knowledgeable solver has no way to know from outside the text whether the size
increase worked here. This residual channel is declared for G-STEM rather than
argued away.

## 8. Declared deviations, and the checks that are vacuous here

**Paragraphs: 4, against the ELF blueprint's authoring target of 1 for
`short_text`.** `gates/bands.json` — the only mechanical authority (law 10) —
allows 0–8 for `short_text`, which is the measured corpus range, so M-BANDS
passes and the blueprint's “1” is an authoring convention rather than a
calibrated band. The deviation is structural, not stylistic: a passage carrying a
paragraph of direct quoted speech (the binding lane constraint) cannot run the
narration and the quotation together without the quotation misreading as the
writer's own voice, and the byline is its own line by house convention.

**Readability: not measured, and therefore not asserted.** No `fk_grade`, Flesch
or polysyllabic figure appears anywhere in `generator_meta`, because law 7 puts
readability in G-SPRÅK / G-REGISTER's hands and RULE 19 forbids asserting a
statistic that was not computed on these bytes. For the record, the register is
plainly at the low-grade end of the blueprint's 11.0–15.0 band — the vocabulary
is *net, deck, mesh, twine, bag, drag, haddock, plaice* — which is the same
declared deviation the shipped TYPE-001 engineering shorts carry (b16-003 8.7,
b17-003 7.2, b18-003 8.9, b19-003 7.5).

**RULE 11 (cross-question lexical/conceptual bridges) is vacuous** for a
one-question unit: there is no second option set for a blind solver to
corroborate against. Recorded rather than skipped, because the rule asks for the
check to be stated. The single-question analogue — whether the *stem* leaks into
the option set — is §4 above and
`generator_meta.stem_entailment_audit`.

**RULE 15 (mechanism-inventory paired with a stance question)** likewise cannot
apply: one question, and it is neither an inventory nor a stance item.
