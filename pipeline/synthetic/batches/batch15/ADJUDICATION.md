### Text

In February 2022 a hospital sent Rhiannon Bellinghast’s laboratory a request it could not fill. The patient was a man in his sixties whose knee replacement had been infected for eleven months, through two washouts and four antibiotics, and his surgeons wanted to know whether anything in the laboratory’s freezers would kill the strain he was carrying. Technicians spread his isolate over ninety plates, dropped a different phage onto each one, and waited. A phage is a virus that infects bacteria and nothing else; where it takes hold, the bacteria die in a widening circle and leave a clear spot – a plaque – in the film of growth. Ninety plates came back cloudy. The knee came out in April. Cases that end that way rarely reach print, which is worth saying before any of the numbers that follow.

What Bellinghast’s group has and most hospitals do not is the collection itself: 1,140 phages gathered since 2016 from sewage outfalls, hospital drains and, in one productive week, a duck pond, of which some four hundred have been sequenced and described in full. Each phage fastens onto a particular molecule on the bacterial surface, so a phage that clears one patient’s strain may do nothing whatever to the strain in the next room. There is no broad-spectrum version to fall back on. Over five years the collection has produced a usable match for rather more than half of the isolates sent to it, and that figure hides a spread: close to three in four for Klebsiella, well under half for the staphylococci that dominate infected implants. Bellinghast says the gap has not narrowed as the collection has grown, which is not what she expected when she began adding to it.

The clinical record is thin, and honest about being thin. A series published last year followed sixty-three patients treated at four hospitals between 2020 and 2024; in forty-one the infection was under control at six months, and eleven had the implant taken out anyway. Every patient in it was also on antibiotics, since withholding them would have been indefensible, and the hospitals had referred the cases they judged most likely to respond. “Our results suggest that phages add something in a subset of these infections,” Bellinghast says. “They cannot tell us how large that something is.” She is careful about what the series will bear, and the care is warranted: without a comparison group, a recovery in a patient taking four drugs says little about the fifth. Josephine Quilvey, who treats bone and joint infection at one of the four hospitals, is readier than that. She would offer a matched phage as a standard option in infected joint replacements, where the alternative is often a second operation to take the implant out, and she is deliberate about the limit: she means that setting, not resistant infection at large.

Bacteria adapt to phages much as they adapt to drugs, usually by altering the very molecule the phage has to grip, and the field has a hopeful story about what that alteration costs them. The molecule is often one the bacterium needs as well, so an escape mutant ought to hold less firmly to an implant, or to become vulnerable again to an antibiotic it had shrugged off. Bellinghast’s group sequenced thirty-four escape mutants recovered from patients during treatment. Nine had paid a price of that kind. The other twenty-five clung to plastic and grew as strongly as the strains they came from. Putting several phages into a single preparation makes escape less likely without abolishing it, and every such mixture is made for one named patient, which is where the paperwork starts.

The part of all this that has genuinely improved is unglamorous. In 2021 the median time between a hospital sending an isolate and a preparation coming back was nineteen days, which is longer than most of these decisions can wait; last year it was six, on the strength of a courier contract, a technician on call over the weekend, and a decision to stop fully sequencing a phage before it is used. A randomized trial in prosthetic joint infection is now enrolling at two of the four hospitals, and that trial, not any series, will decide whether the treatment becomes ordinary. The isolate from February 2022 is still in a freezer two floors below the plate room. Two hundred phages have joined the collection since; none of them clears it.
– Neil Hollowmere, science correspondent

En namnsdag krävde ingenting särskilt av den som ville uppvakta. Datumet stod i almanackan, lika för alla som bar namnet, och man behövde varken fråga någon eller minnas något privat. Etnologen Algot Rimhall har läst veckopressens frågespalter från mellankrigstiden och framåt, och hans slutsats vänder på en vanlig föreställning. Man brukar anta att gåvan hölls liten därför att dagen i sig redan var obetydlig. Rimhall menar det omvända: dagen kunde förbli liten just därför att taket höll. Så länge ingen kunde överträffa någon annan blev uppvaktningen aldrig en tävling, och en uppvaktning som inte är en tävling kan omfatta hur många som helst. Han går varsamt fram i formuleringarna, eftersom spaltsvar är ett skevt material, men riktningen är han säker på.

Att almanackan förlorade sin självklara plats i hemmen spelade förstås roll: färre hushåll hade en att slå upp, och namnsdagslängden gjordes dessutom om ett par gånger, så att den som ville gratulera inte längre kunde lita på minnet. Det som förändrades var att taket upphörde att vara självklart. När en bukett kunde kosta vad som helst blev också den minsta uppvaktningen ett val, och val kräver att man rangordnar sina bekanta.
