## Counterintuitive direction and dense residue

Lay priors read a tall ornate chimney pot as **display** — the grand pot over
the grand room. The passage's mechanism runs the other way: the pot standing a
course above its neighbours belongs to the flue that **smoked**, usually the
**shortest** flue on the stack, the attic bedroom's, because draught scales
with the height of the warm column beneath it. So the tallest pot marks the
meanest room and the worst flue. Residue the item never tests: the separate
grate behind each pot, the yards' stock, the 1961 rebuild, the narrowed mouth
speeding the gases, the fillet of mortar, the sweep who could try a taller pot
without a bricklayer.

**Law 9 (no manufactured tidiness):** the decorative explanation is real and is
conceded, not strawmanned — three competing non-diagnostic causes of pattern
(yard stock, householder display, a later rebuild) are crowded into one long
sentence before the pivot. The passage never claims ornament is meaningless,
only that it does not tell you which flue misbehaved.

## Fact base (full log in `generator_meta.mechanism_verification_note`)

Run **before** drafting, after the batch15 short was refuted at V-FINAL for a
backwards mechanism.

- **Narrowing speeds the gases** — as flue gases pass the narrowed outlet of a
  pot their velocity rises (continuity / Venturi), improving draught, often
  more effectively than the same height in plain masonry.
- **Several pots on one stack** = several fireplaces on different floors
  sharing the stack.
- **The decorative counter-explanation is real** and is why the passage
  concedes rather than dismisses: the Victorian era was the "Golden Age" of
  chimney pots largely because identical terraces led householders to
  personalise them; 300+ designs, strong regional naming.
- **Height compensates for poor draw** — where a roofline or obstruction puts
  the terminal in the turbulence zone, extra height is the standard fix
  (3-2-10 rule); taller pots are used in closely built settings.
- **Draught scales with the warm column** — stack effect: buoyancy rises with
  column height and thermal difference; a pot raises the outlet, lengthens the
  column of hot gases and enhances natural draught.
- **Flaunching** — the mortar bedding at the head of the stack into which pots
  are bedded; a pot is replaced by taking off the failed flaunching, bedding
  the new pot and re-forming the fillet, with the brickwork below left alone.
- **NEGATIVE RESULT, logged not buried.** A WebFetch of a trade page titled
  *"The Height of Chimney Pots: Why They Vary and Why It Matters"* confirmed
  only generic claims and **explicitly did not address within-one-stack height
  variation**, whether a taller pot fixes poor draw, flaunching, or outlet
  narrowing. **The within-stack inference is therefore mine**, built from
  documented physics (draught scales with warm-column height; the shortest flue
  has the shortest column), not quoted from a source that states it in those
  words. Flagged here for pedagogy-review and V-FINAL rather than presented as
  sourced. The softest sentence in the passage — that *a sweep* could try a
  taller pot without a bricklayer — is a trade claim about who does the work,
  and no option turns on it.
