The visitors' gallery on the eighty-first floor of the Verrand Tower sells more tickets than the observation deck above it, and the reason is a ball of layered steel plate the width of a bus, hung on eight cables in a well cut through the building. It is a tuned mass damper. When the wind comes hard off the estuary the ball swings — late, heavily, half a beat behind the tower — and takes the sway out of the floors around it. Visitors photograph it; brochures give it a number. At commissioning, the Verrand's damper cut peak acceleration at the top of the building by two-fifths. That figure is honest, and it is the figure the whole argument about tall buildings and wind has been conducted in for thirty years. It describes one afternoon.

Hedda Rask has been collecting the afternoons since. For twelve years her group has kept accelerometers running near the top of nineteen towers in Brayle, sampling continuously rather than in the week of handover tests, and matching the record against the complaint logs floor by floor. On the commissioning figure the dampers did what was claimed of them. In eighteen of the nineteen towers the reduction measured in the first year sat within a percentage point or two of what the designers had predicted, and in the buildings where trouble had been expected — the slender ones, on exposed corners — the complaints did not arrive. Rask says this part of the record is not in dispute. The dampers work, and they work about as well as the brochures say.

What the twelve-year record shows is what happens next. A tuned mass damper is tuned: its mass and its cable length are set against the tower's own natural frequency, and it does its work only in a narrow band around that frequency. Buildings do not stay put. Fit-out adds partitions, tenants add paper and machinery, mechanical rooms take on water, and every addition of mass drags the frequency down. Across the nineteen towers the frequency measured in year ten sat on average some four percent below the value the damper had been set against, and in the three most heavily fitted-out buildings nearer seven. The benefit fell with it. By year eight the median tower was getting rather less than half the reduction it had been commissioned with — not because anything had broken, but because the building had walked away from its own damper. Where a damper was retuned the benefit came back almost in full. Four of the nineteen were retuned.

Towers that manage the wind by their shape do not drift. A profile that tapers, corners that are chamfered or notched, a slot cut through the upper floors: these break up the regular vortex shedding that drives crosswind sway, and they go on breaking it up for as long as the building stands, unattended and unbilled. Constantin Marek, who designed the notched profile of the Ossian, puts it without qualification. "A damper is a maintenance contract you have signed on behalf of everyone who owns the building after you," he says. "A shape is a decision made once. One of those survives a change of owner. The other does not." Rask allows him the first half. "A shape is tuned too," she says. "Not to a frequency — to a wind." The modeling assumes the directions the wind arrives from and the ground it crosses on the way in; when a forty-story block went up two hundred meters upwind of the Halberd, the accelerometers there recorded a rise in crosswind response that has not gone away. Nobody can notch the Halberd now.

So the damper's advantage decays and can be restored; the shape's advantage does not decay and cannot be. Neither is obviously the safer bet, which may be why the choice so often gets made on the commissioning figure instead: it is available early, it fits in a brochure, and it says nothing about year ten. Rask will not tell an owner which to build. An honest comparison, she thinks, would have to name a maintenance regime alongside the percentage, and the blocks not yet built alongside that — and nobody can do the second. Wind engineering is sold as a problem solved at the ribbon-cutting. It is a problem that has to be kept solved, by somebody, for as long as the tower stands and the skyline around it goes on moving.

Att en bemannad lokal skulle lösa allt tror jag inte. En bibliotekarie kan varken krympa klasserna eller skapa den tid lärare behöver för att planera tillsammans med någon. En skola med sextio elever kan dessutom svårligen bära en heltidstjänst; där måste lösningen se annorlunda ut.
