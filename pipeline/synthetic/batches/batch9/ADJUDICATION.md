Ask anyone who has argued for the return of beavers to a British headwater what the animals are for, and the answer comes back in cubic metres. A dam ponds water; a chain of dams ponds a great deal of it; and water sitting in a pond is water not running towards the town at the bottom of the valley. The arithmetic is easy to carry in the head, and it has done more for the case than any argument about willow ever did. Storage is the figure quoted at consultations, printed on posters and totted up in hectare-metres by volunteers with tape measures. What it assumes is that a flood peak is made of volume. It is made of timing.

That assumption has now been put to a catchment. Over nine years the hydrologist Anneke Sorrel and her group followed eleven headwater sub-catchments of the Ferrow into which beavers had returned at different dates, keeping the rainfall record and the gauging method common to all of them and letting the dams accumulate as the colonies saw fit. On the figure the leaflets quote, the result was everything the campaigners had promised. Dam count tracked storage almost exactly, storage rose year on year, and in the ordinary winter storms — the ones that come six or seven times a winter — the peak that reached the gauge at Millstake was lower than before the beavers, and it arrived later. That held in all eleven sub-catchments, the steep peaty ones and the gentle grazed ones alike.

Then Sorrel's group stopped counting ordinary storms and looked at the rare ones. A defence at the bottom of a valley is not built for the rain that comes six times a season but for the storm that comes once in a working life, and it was in those that stored volume stopped predicting anything. Across the fourteen largest events in the record, the drop in peak height at Millstake bore almost no relation to how much water the dams upstream could hold. What it tracked instead was where those dams were. Where colonies had spread across several tributaries, the peak was blunted more often than not; where a similar volume of pond sat in one long chain on a single tributary, it was not, and in two of the fourteen the gauge read a little higher than it had in the pre-beaver years. Sorrel will not push the explanation far. The tributaries of the Ferrow are of very unequal length, she points out, and water entering the highest of them takes the better part of a day longer to reach Millstake than water entering the lowest; a chain of ponds on one tributary fills, spills and empties on a single schedule. There is a ceiling on it in any case. Once the rain has run long enough for every pond in a sub-catchment to fill — about forty hours in the Ferrow — the freeboard is gone, and the water is passed down much as it would have been before the animals came back.

This is not how the argument is usually conducted. Douglas Traer, who has campaigned for a decade to get beavers into the next valley, has no patience for qualifications. "Every dam is a flood defence, and every pond is money the council does not have to spend on concrete," he told me. "Engineers have had two centuries and a great deal of public money, and the town still floods. Put the animals back and stand out of the way." Sorrel is more careful. Where the dams end up can be nudged, she says, by fencing, by planting and by leaving some tributaries to themselves — but the gain against the largest storms is modest, and the window in which it works is narrower than any leaflet admits.

Read whole, the Ferrow record is more divided than either side's leaflet. It does not make the animals decorative: in the ordinary storms the ponds do the obvious work, and the reduction is real enough for the people at the bottom of the valley to feel it in a wet January. What it unsettles is the figure the argument has been conducted in. Volume is easy to measure, easy to add up and easy to put on a poster, and when the water is high it predicts very little. How the dams are spread across a catchment's tributaries is awkward to measure and predicts a good deal. On the Ferrow, at least, what the beavers did with a flood was not so much hold it back as take it apart.
