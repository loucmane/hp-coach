There are three hundred and forty pans on the Aveste flats, each about the size of a tennis court, floored with grey clay that is trodden flat again every spring. Sea water enters the chain at the north end in March and takes eleven weeks to reach the last pond, losing four fifths of itself on the way. What is left is raked out with a wooden lath, dried in open sheds and sold unwashed. Two hundred and twelve of the pans belong to the cooperative that registered the Aveste mark in 2014; the rest are worked by two families who never joined and sell under no name at all. The mark is precise about equipment. It names the lath, keeps metal out of the pan, bars washing and heat-drying, and sets out how the sheds are ventilated. Salt that carries it fetches about six times the price of the bagged industrial grade.

Iria Solbes has spent five seasons on the flats trying to establish which of those rules is doing the work. She is a brine chemist with a laboratory in the old customs house and a standing arrangement with eleven pan-holders, and her method is unglamorous: take salt from one pond under two conditions and put both before a panel that is never told which is which. Over five seasons the panels separated nothing the mark regulates. Lath-raked salt and salt lifted with a plastic scoop came out level, and so did clay against the three pans relined with concrete in 2019. What the panels did separate, in every season and by a wide margin, was dwell — the number of days the brine had stood in the final pond before the salt was taken off it. Salt lifted at three days carried roughly a fifth less magnesium than salt from the same pond lifted at eight, the bittern having had that much longer to concentrate. "A panel tells you what a mouth can find, and a mouth is not a stable instrument," Solbes says. "But that one came back five times, and the rules we protect never came back at all."

None of which is an argument against the lath, and Solbes is careful to say so. Dwell can be got right at Aveste because a lath and a plank can be carried out at any hour of any day, by one person, at an hour's notice. The saltern at Pella Marsa, forty kilometres up the coast, was floored in the 1990s to take a small harvester, and its dates are settled before the season opens, because the contractor who owns the machine works six sites between March and September. Teodor Panek, who chairs the certifying body, has read all of it and disputes none of it. His objection is procedural. "You can photograph a lath," he says. "You cannot photograph a decision about whether the crust is ready." Solbes agrees with every word of that and has said so in print. Then she points to the concrete. On chemistry the three relined pans behave like the clay ones. By 2021 they had the new floors by heart and the lateness stopped. Nothing her panels could find in the salt recorded the relining. The people did.

Solbes has asked for one more line on the certificate, a dwell window of three to five days in the final pond, and has not got it. The committee has not refused it either.
