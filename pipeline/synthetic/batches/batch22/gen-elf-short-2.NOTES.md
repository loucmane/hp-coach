## 1. Why this subject

The lane brief bars an institutional record with counts, because the two units
before it were the same essay twice: elf-b20-004 read an enrolment register and
counted second bindings, elf-b21-004 read a loan ledger and counted
fourteenth-day returns. Six of the seven shipped history-essay shorts are in
fact parish or trade administration read through its own paperwork (turnpike
side-bar, village pound, milk round, pew rent, receiving house, and the two
above), so moving the *evidence type* was not enough on its own; the domain had
to move too.

What this unit uses instead, from the list the brief allows: **a dispute
preserved in letters**, plus **the ground itself** and **a route pegged across
it**. Nothing is counted. The passage contains no tally of entries, loans,
returns, subscribers or people — its numerals are four dates (1845, 1839, 1846,
1851) and nothing else, and its one duration is anchored to an event (*since the
enclosure*) rather than to a round number, which also keeps it clear of the
brief's bar on a "fifteen springs"-style loose count.

The argument is a historian's move that does not need arithmetic: **the object
of a quarrel is identified by geography and by a concession.** Ruddiforth's
attorney claims a stretch of hedge running *from the stone gap down to the
lane*; a surveyor had pegged a railway line *along the bank, from the stone gap
to the lane end* the month before. Same two landmarks, same strip. And in
February Ruddiforth offers to give up the timber if he can keep the ground —
which is a strange trade for a man who wants a hedge.

**Setting.** Inland, agricultural, English, 1840s. Huntingdonshire is a real
historic county used generically, with nothing invented attached to it; it was
chosen partly because the bank's English units already cluster in Lincolnshire
(18 files), Northamptonshire (10), Warwickshire (7) and Shropshire (5), and
Huntingdonshire appears in none. That disclosure is repeated in
`generator_meta.named_figures` so a later registry refill does not mistake it
for an omitted coinage.

## 2. Passage architecture

Nine sentences, one paragraph, byline inside the string (law 6).

1. A five-word verdict — *Until 1845 the hedge had never been in dispute.*
2. The longest sentence in the unit: what the hedge was and how the two farms
   had worked it.
3. The claim, with its extent. **Key leg 1.**
4. What the letters argue about (a gate, some stones) and the one thing the
   attorneys agree on — the 1839 laying. **Refutes B.**
5. The tracing of the deposited plan, and who enclosed it. **Refutes A.**
6. The surveyor's line, with its extent — reported *after* the letters, out of
   chronological order, so the passage never joins it to sentence 3.
   **Key leg 2.**
7. The February offer. **Corroborates D.**
8. The scheme abandoned, no land taken. **Refutes C.**
9. Coda: the hedge laid again in 1851 by both farms together.

**RULE 24 compliance.** No received-explanation opening. No record's-silence
motif anywhere: the passage never says a document fails to record something —
every negative in it is a statement about what happened (*never been in
dispute*, *no land was taken*), not about what the papers omit. No labour-drain
motif, no institution-in-decline, no money arithmetic (there is not one sum in
the unit). The coda is **concrete and resolved**, which is one of the coda types
the batch needs after five of seven batch21 units closed deliberately
unresolved.

**Residue (law 9).** The gate and the heap of stones the letters also quarrel
over, the ditch cleaned from below and the thorn cut from above, the enclosure
that put the bank there, and the 1851 laying. None of it points at the answer,
and the quarrel is never adjudicated: the passage does not say who owned the
bank.

## 3. The one question

> What is implied about the quarrel between the neighbouring farms?

Corpus-attested stem form (*What is implied about …* is the dominant ELF
inference stem, 11 bare instances plus many object-specific ones in the 405
authentic ELF stems). It names the quarrel and predicates nothing about it, so
it entails no option (RULE 12).

| | option | variable | fate |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | plan came into the correspondence from Ruddiforth's attorney | provenance of the plan | **refuted**: *The neighbour's attorney enclosed a tracing of the railway company's deposited plan.* |
| B | thorn had gone unlaid for a generation | the hedge's upkeep | **refuted**: *both sides agree the thorn was last laid in 1839* against the November 1845 date — six years |
| C | railway afterwards carried through the two farms | fate of the scheme | **refuted**: *The scheme was given up in the autumn of 1846, and no land was taken.* |
| **D** | **the ground claimed was the ground the surveyor had pegged out** | **extent of the claim** | **key** |

Traps: A is a role/attribution swap over a true detail (surface-word match —
the tracing really is in the file, on the wrong side); B is the ready-made
outside-knowledge story that a neglected hedge causes a boundary quarrel; C is
plausible world knowledge, that a pegged line gets built. Every one of the three
dies on a span the rationale cites, rather than being merely unsupported
(RULE 23).

**Option-form self-test — the six G-STEM routes:**

1. *Absolutizer / hedge triage.* No option carries an M-FORM absolutizer and no
   option is modalised. RULE 23's balance is met by the **none** branch: zero
   hedged options, not exactly one. The key is a flat, unhedged assertion, so
   "pick the qualified one" has nothing to select (law 10 / rule 10).
2. *Contradictory dyad.* None — four different predicates, no did/did-not pair.
   An earlier draft had a distractor asserting Ruddiforth dropped the claim
   *once the company gave up the line*, which presupposed the withdrawal another
   option denied; it was rebuilt for exactly that reason.
3. *Mirrored twin.* No reversed-causality mirror.
4. *Main-idea scope triage.* Not a main-idea item; all four options are the same
   shape and within 13–16 tokens of one another.
5. *Stem announces the shape.* The stem says *implied*, so at least two options
   are inference-shaped: B needs the 1839/1845 arithmetic, D needs the two
   extents matched. "Pick the one that needs a step" does not pick the key.
6. *RULE 15 mechanisms.* Vacuous for a one-question unit; likewise RULE 11.

## 4. Blind solve

**From the passage alone, before consulting my own key.** A fails on the
sentence naming the neighbour's attorney. B fails on 1839 against November 1845
— six years, which for a thorn bank on a laying cycle is ordinary husbandry.
C fails on the abandonment and *no land was taken*. D survives: the claim and
the pegged line run between the same two landmarks, and the February concession
points the same way. **Exactly one defensible answer.**

**With the passage covered, ranking on wording alone.** Same shape, same length
class, no hedge, no absolutiser, no contradictory pair, four unrelated
predicates: **1 in 4**, no better. The one residual surface pattern is that
three options mention the railway, the plan or the surveyor and B does not — an
odd-one-out reader therefore lands on **B, a distractor**, so the pattern does
not select the key. Declared floor 0.25.
