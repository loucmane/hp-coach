### Text

A contrail that lasts is mostly not the airplane’s doing. The engine puts out heat, water vapor and a great many specks of soot, and if the air the plume mixes into is cold enough, the mixture passes briefly through the point at which liquid water condenses; droplets form on the specks and freeze almost at once. That much is over in seconds. Everything after it belongs to the air the airplane happened to be crossing. Usually that air is dry with respect to ice, the crystals hand their water back, and the line behind the wing is gone inside a minute or two. Sometimes it is not dry. In a layer already carrying more vapor than ice can hold at that temperature, the crystals do not shrink but feed, and go on feeding, so that by the time a contrail is an hour old most of the ice in it was never inside an engine. It came out of the air the airplane was passing through.

The awkward part is that a layer like that has nothing in it to look at. Air can sit well past ice saturation and make no cloud of its own, because starting a crystal from nothing needs roughly half again as much vapor as keeping an existing one alive. So the sky stays clear, the layer runs a couple of hundred miles with a depth that would fit inside a small hill, and the first anyone knows of it is the mark an airplane leaves crossing it. The forecasts are not much help either. Temperature at altitude is predicted well. Humidity at altitude is predicted from almost nothing, because almost nothing up there measures it, and the models were built for weather that reaches the ground.

Two winters ago a control center began moving airplanes anyway. On nights when the forecast laid one of these layers across a route, the flight was planned two thousand feet beneath it, and the crews were told why. Nights only: a spreading contrail holds in heat that would otherwise leave, and in daylight it also turns some sunlight back toward space, so the two effects pull against one another, while after dark there is nothing pulling the other way. Sunniva Vestrigg, who built the forecast the center used, is unsentimental about what it can do. “The layers are usually shallow enough that one step down is out of them,” she says. “Which night, and which fifty miles of the route – that is what we get wrong.”

Anselm Colverdine audits what the airplanes actually did, and his numbers are the least comfortable part of the record. Of 141 flights that were left alone and flew straight through a forecast layer, 61 left a mark anybody could find afterward. The forecast was calling for wet air a good deal more often than the sky supplied it. Of 406 plans written with the step-down in them, 96 were flown that way; the rest were traded away for traffic, for weather, for a crew that wanted the fuel. An adjusted airplane burned about two percent more than it would have. Spread over everything the sector handled that winter, the extra came to a fraction of a percent. “The maneuver is the cheap part,” Colverdine says. “It was always going to be the cheap part.”

Which leaves the counting. A contrail cannot be seen at the moment it is made; it needs anywhere from five minutes to an hour to spread wide enough for a satellite to register it, and by then the wind has carried it well away from where it was born. The picture is flat, so nothing in it says how high the thing is. On a busy evening four or five tracks cross the same square of sky. And a success looks like an empty picture. So does a forecast that was wrong, and a contrail too thin to register, and a sheet of cloud sitting over the top of one. A picture that shows a contrail settles that one flight. An empty one settles nothing, and only one of the readings it allows blames the forecast; two are the camera’s, and the fourth is the success the flight was after. That the forecast asks for more than the sky supplies is the best of those readings, and the forecast’s own author reads it the same way, but it is a reading of the empty frames and not a count taken from them. Vestrigg wants humidity sensors flying on the airliners themselves, a hundred of them to begin with, and believes the rest would follow. Perhaps it would. But the satellite count is the smallest thing those two winters produced. Colverdine is right about the cheap part, and the findings that will outlast the trial are his: that the forecast asks for more descents than the sky calls for, and that most of the descents it asks for are never flown. The second of those comes off records the center already keeps and needed no picture at all. A third winter is being flown. The sensors have not been fitted.
– Lorcan Trawsfield

## 3. AmE/BrE-sömmen i citattypografin — kosmetisk, och den ligger mellan skikten
