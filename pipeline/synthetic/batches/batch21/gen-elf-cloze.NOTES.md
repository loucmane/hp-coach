# gen-elf-cloze (batch21) — generator notes

**Unit:** ELF cloze, 5 gaps. `family` =
`ELF-CLOZE-001 / village-hall-jumble-sale-last-hour-society-commentary-cloze`.
**Title:** Fill a Bag. **Byline:** bare, `– Wendy Bowles`. **Genre:**
society_commentary. **Spelling:** BrE, held throughout. **Keys:** D C B B A.

Assigned subject: the last hour of a village-hall jumble sale — what is left on
the tables, who is still there, what the price of everything drops to, and one
real idea about what people leave behind. Inland. Bare byline. The theme *a
written instrument that fails to govern behaviour* is excluded and is not used.

**The real idea.** The obvious reading of a picked-over table is that the
village looked at the things and said no. The piece refuses that and puts an
arrival-time mechanism in its place: boxes come in all day, the ones that come
in after two are the ones their owners have been circling all week, and a box
that arrived at three has had no worse a case than one that arrived at ten — it
has simply been on the trestle for an hour instead of a day. So what is left at
five is sorted by hour of arrival at least as much as by taste, and the things a
household found hardest to give up are the things most likely to be left. The
piece does not close the case: the organiser names exceptions in both directions
before anybody asks, and she is wrong about one guess in four.

## 3. Per-gap self-blind-solve

Method: passage only, no rationales, arguing **for** each non-keyed option
before ruling it out, and then covering the passage to see whether the option
set can be ranked on wording alone (the G-STEM option-form test).

### Gap 1 — `earned / gained / returned / fetched`, key **D**
Frame: *A jacket that would have ___ two pounds at half past ten goes at three
for whatever coin is held out.*

- **FOR A (earned):** the whole afternoon is about money coming in, and *earn* is
  the commonest verb for money coming in; a reader who has just read *the
  takings* two sentences later will reach for it. **Against:** an inanimate
  subject cannot earn — a person, a stall or a business earns; a jacket does not.
- **FOR B (gained):** *gain* is a plain acquisition verb and a solver may take
  *gained two pounds* as neutral for realised two pounds. **Against:** *gain*
  takes an abstract object (ground, weight, an advantage, time), not a sale
  price; and it fails on the same inanimate subject.
- **FOR C (returned):** in an investment sense a holding *returns* a sum, and a
  solver reading the piece as being about what things are worth may take the
  jacket as the asset. **Against:** that use requires a yield over time from
  capital, not a one-morning price on a trestle.
- **FOR D (fetched):** *a thing fetches a price* is the fixed second-hand and
  auction collocation, and it is the only one of the four that takes an
  inanimate subject with a money object.
- **Verdict:** single-answerable. Covered-passage ranking: all four are ordinary
  past participles of money verbs, none carries a hedge or an absolutiser, and
  none is longest — nothing to rank on but the collocation.

### Gap 2 — `keep / kept / keeping / to keep`, key **C**
Frame: *Whether the hall is worth ___ open for a last hour is not decided on the
takings.*

- **FOR A (keep):** a solver who has settled the meaning first will drop in the
  bare form. **Against:** *worth keep open* is not English in any register.
- **FOR B (kept):** *worth* does take a participle in some frames a learner half
  remembers (*worth said*, *worth doing*), so a past participle looks safe.
  **Against:** *kept* here would need a passive subject and cannot govern the
  object complement *open*.
- **FOR D (to keep):** this is the strongest wrong answer and should collect most
  of the errors: *worth* patterns semantically with *able*, *ready*, *fit*, all
  of which take the infinitive. **Against:** *worth* is one of the few English
  words that refuses it and takes a gerund only.
- **FOR C (keeping):** *worth keeping open* is the licensed form.
- **Verdict:** single-answerable on the complement rule alone. Covered-passage
  ranking: four forms of one ordinary verb; no semantic ranking exists.

### Gap 3 — `clearance / giveaway / markdown / liquidation`, key **B**
Frame: *The saucers of coins go to one end of the trestle, nothing has a price
after that, and the afternoon moves from a sale to a ___.*

- **FOR A (clearance):** the tables are literally being cleared, and *clearance*
  is the natural retail word for the end of a run of stock. It is the strongest
  distractor. **Against:** a clearance **is** a sale, with prices on the goods,
  so an afternoon cannot move *from a sale to* one; and the clause immediately
  before removes the prices.
- **FOR C (markdown):** the piece has just tracked a price falling from two
  pounds to a coin, which is what a markdown is. **Against:** a markdown is a
  reduced *price*, and the frame has just said nothing has a price; it is also
  shop vocabulary in a hall with saucers of coins on a trestle.
- **FOR D (liquidation):** the sale is winding up and the goods are being
  disposed of, which is roughly what the word means. **Against:** it is the
  winding up of a company and its assets, wrong in register and wrong in sense
  at once.
- **FOR B (giveaway):** the only one of the four that names an event at which
  nothing is charged, which is what the two preceding clauses have established.
- **Verdict:** single-answerable. Covered-passage ranking: four singular count
  nouns for a downward turn in price or trade; no length, hedge or polarity tell.

### Gap 4 — `amply / briefly / thinly / openly`, key **B**
Frame: *Those are on the trestles at five not because the village refused them
but because they were ___ offered.*

- **FOR D (openly):** *openly offered* is live English and the sentence can be
  read as *they were on show and still passed over*. This is the polarity mirror
  and the reason the gap works. **Against:** a thing passed over after being on
  show **has** been refused, which is precisely the reading the *not because …
  but because* frame has just excluded.
- **FOR A (amply):** the piece is about how much of a thing there was, so a
  quantity adverb looks apt. **Against:** *amply offered* is not an English
  combination — *amply* modifies supply, provision, reward — and it sits at the
  wrong end of the scale.
- **FOR C (thinly):** small-scale, which is the right end of the scale, and the
  passage does describe a covering that is *low and even*. **Against:** *thinly*
  measures spread, not duration; goods on a trestle are not *thinly offered*.
- **FOR B (briefly):** small-scale **and** the only one of the small-scale pair
  that combines with *offered*; the paragraph has already named time on the
  table as the quantity in question.
- **Verdict:** single-answerable, and it needs both moves: the pole removes two
  (openly, amply) and the collocation removes two (amply, thinly). Covered-
  passage ranking: four `-ly` adverbs, suffix-rhymed, two at each end of the
  scale, one live collocation in each pair — nothing rankable on shape.

### Gap 5 — `Still / Granted / Therefore / Occasionally`, key **A**
Frame: previous sentence names two exceptions; gapped sentence returns to the
claim and says how far it holds (*she can walk the tables at five and say which
hour a thing came in, and be wrong about one in four*).

- **FOR B (Granted):** the paragraph is visibly about exceptions, and *Granted*
  is the standard opener for conceding one; a solver who registers only the
  topic will take it. **Against:** the concession is in the **previous**
  sentence. Putting *Granted* in front of the claim makes the claim the thing
  conceded, which reverses the paragraph. This is the near-synonym pair, and the
  discriminating fact is in the text, not in the words.
- **FOR C (Therefore):** two named examples in front of a general statement look
  like grounds for it. **Against:** they are counterexamples; a list of things a
  generalisation fails to fit does not produce that generalisation.
- **FOR D (Occasionally):** the sentence is about how often something works, and
  a frequency marker looks at home. **Against:** it collides with what follows,
  which is what she can do on any day plus a stated error rate — *occasionally*
  would make the whole ability intermittent, which the sentence contradicts.
- **FOR A (Still):** marks a claim surviving a concession already made, which is
  exactly the relation between the two sentences.
- **Verdict:** single-answerable. Covered-passage ranking: four one-word markers,
  four different discourse relations, none a hedge, none an absolutiser, all
  taking the same comma-plus-clause frame.

**Adversarial blind floor for the unit: 1-in-4 on every gap.** No gap can be
narrowed below the full field on option form alone.

## 4. The three gap-craft checks, per gap (honestly)

The three lessons from batch20's cloze, which took three flags on gap form even
after passing G-REGISTER as the batch's most diversified unit:
(1) no bound antonymic dyad; (2) no lone opposite-polarity option, and every
option collocationally live for the same head at the same polarity;
(3) no near-synonym pair inviting twin-cancellation unless the sentence carries
the discriminating fact explicitly.

| gap | (1) bound antonymic dyad? | (2) lone-polarity outlier / 3+1 substitutability? | (3) near-synonym pair straddling the key? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No. earned / gained / returned / fetched are four money verbs; no pair are opposites. | No. All four are positive-polarity money verbs with an inanimate subject; none is a lone outlier. **Honest caveat:** *earned* and *gained* are the closest pair and a solver could cancel both, leaving two — but they cancel each other as **distractors**, and *returned* is not substitutable with either, so the field does not halve on a single axis. | No. The near-synonym pair (earned/gained) sits wholly among the distractors; the key is distinct from both. |
| 2 | No. Four forms of one verb are not opposites. | Not applicable — no polarity axis. All four are live forms of KEEP; each is grammatical somewhere, none anywhere else in this frame. | No. **Honest note:** *to keep* and *keeping* are morphologically adjacent, and the discriminating fact is the complement rule, which is fully explicit in the frame (*worth …*). That is what a grammar gap is. |
| 3 | No. clearance / giveaway / markdown / liquidation are four kinds of downward turn, not two opposed pairs. | No. All four are the same polarity (prices going down or trade ending). **Honest caveat:** *clearance* and *markdown* are the retail pair and both die on the same explicit fact — that the prices are gone — so the field can narrow to two (giveaway, liquidation) for a reader who spots that fact. It cannot narrow further without register, and it cannot narrow at all without reading the clause. | No. *clearance* is the nearest to the key in feel, not in sense; the sentence supplies the discriminator (a clearance is a kind of **sale**, and the sentence moves *from a sale*). |
| 4 | No, and this is the gap that was **rebuilt** for it. The first draft ran `more / shorter / less / longer`, which is two clean dyads and hands a solver 1-in-2 on shape alone. Nothing is a lone outlier and nothing is 3-substitutable-plus-1. | No. No two of the four are near-synonyms; the four span quantity, duration, spread and visibility. |
| 5 | No. Four different discourse relations, no opposed pair. | No polarity axis. The sentence carries the discriminating fact explicitly — the concession is in the **preceding** sentence, so the gapped sentence cannot itself be the concession. This is stated as a live risk, not waved away: it is the one place in the unit where a determined solver could argue, and it is argued down from the text rather than from register. |

**Exotic-distractor check.** Every one of the twenty options is a word a learner
has met: earned, gained, returned, fetched; keep, kept, keeping, to keep;
clearance, giveaway, markdown, liquidation; amply, briefly, thinly, openly;
Still, Granted, Therefore, Occasionally. Nothing on the b20 `scantily` level.

**Option-string reuse.** `widely` was in the gap-4 set until the final check
found it is a shipped option string in elf-b20-002 (gap 5). Replaced with
`openly`, which keeps a live positive-pole collocation with *offered*. No other
option string in this unit has been used in any shipped cloze unit.

**Agreement / morphology checked across ALL FOUR options of every gap**, not
asserted: gap 1 four past participles after one perfect auxiliary; gap 2 four
forms of one verb, deliberately not interchangeable, which is the gap; gap 3
four singular count nouns after one indefinite article; gap 4 four `-ly` adverbs
before one past participle; gap 5 four one-word openers before one comma and one
finite clause.

**Hedge balance (rule 10).** 0 of 5 keys are selected by the
pick-the-qualified-option heuristic. Gaps 1–3 have no hedging axis; gap 4 is a
scale on which two options sit at the small end, so the heuristic narrows to two
and picks nothing; gap 5 is the break in the other direction — *Occasionally* is
the cautious, narrow-scope option and it is **wrong**, while the key lets a flat
general claim stand. No option anywhere carries an M-FORM absolutiser (M-FORM
returns zero findings).

**RULE 11 (cross-question bridges).** Content lemmas appearing in ≥2 option
sets: **none** (checked programmatically after lemmatising every option token).
The only token shared between sets is the function word *to*. Every option word
is also absent from the tokenised title and passage, so no gap is answerable by
surface-matching. Two near-misses were removed in drafting for exactly this:
the passage read *thin and even* (against gap-4 `thinly`) and *are still
covered* (against gap-5 key `Still`); rewritten to *low and even* and *covered
end to end*.

**RULE 12 (stem entailment).** Every prompt is the bare label `Gap (n)`, which
predicates nothing and cannot entail an option.
