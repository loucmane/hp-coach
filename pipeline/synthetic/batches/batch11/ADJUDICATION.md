Kelvain is nineteen years old and has a list. Ninety hectares of black tephra at the northern end of the Sarnhold Rise, still steaming at two vents, and a cumulative record of everything found on it since the sea stopped boiling: sixty-one vascular plants, forty-odd invertebrates, nine breeding birds. The list is reissued after every summer survey, and it has never once been shorter than the year before. That is not a compliment to Kelvain. A cumulative list is a ratchet. It registers arrivals and has no way at all of registering departures, so the figure that reaches the newspapers each autumn is a count of everything the island has ever held rather than of anything it holds now.

Ilse Draeven has been going out to the Rise for nineteen summers, to Kelvain and to six younger islands around it, and she counts differently: not what is new, but what is still there. On the question of arrival her surveys support the textbook almost exactly. Spores and dust-fine seeds came first, carried on the wind within a season or two of the ground cooling; drift-line plants followed, thrown up on the strandline in mats of weed; the bird-carried species arrived last and slowest. That sequence held on six of the seven islands, and the totals recorded in the first decade sat close to what had been forecast for ground of that age and that distance from the mainland. Draeven says this part of the record is not in dispute. New islands are colonized in roughly the order and at roughly the pace the textbooks give.

What nineteen years show, and one year cannot, is how much of that leaves again. Of the sixty-one plant species on Kelvain's list, nineteen were found in each of the last three surveys. The rest are gone, some of them after a single season: the median stay for a wind-arrived plant on Kelvain works out to a little over three years, and for the fine-seeded composites that crowd the early lists it is shorter still. The standing flora of the island has not grown since about its twelfth year. The published number has grown every year. Both of those are true, and only one of them is ever printed, because a ratchet cannot report a loss even when the loss is most of what has happened.

Why anything stays is a harder question than why anything comes, and it is here that Draeven and the volcanologists part company. Petar Ruhe, who has mapped the Rise since before Kelvain existed, holds that the biology is downstream of the rock and always will be. Fresh tephra is loose, and waves take it. An island outlives its eruption only if the ash is hot and fine enough to react with seawater and set into palagonite, and that is settled in the first months, by nothing that later flies in. "An island turns to rock in its first winter, or it is a shoal with a name," Ruhe says. "Vessa had eleven species. Vessa is a depth sounding." Draeven grants him the half of it she thinks is his: nothing colonizes a shoal, and an uncemented island never gets an ecology to lose. But cementation is a fact about the shore rather than the surface. Kelvain's rim is armored and its interior is still largely bare tephra, and almost every one of the nineteen surviving plants stands inside the two hectares under the petrel colony, on ground the birds have been manuring for a decade. Petrels make soil faster than weather does. Petrels are also tenants. When a storm took the headland off Odren's south cape, the colony there moved to a stack four kilometers away, and the plants its nitrogen had been feeding thinned by half within four seasons. Odren's published list did not move at all.

So an island is reported as an event. It surfaces, it is named, it is counted, and the counting is done with an instrument that cannot run backward. Draeven would like the arrival list printed beside two other things: a median residence time, and the name of whatever is currently supplying the island's nitrogen. The second is usually a colony of birds with better options elsewhere. She will not say what Kelvain will look like at fifty. What she will say is that the island is not finished, that nothing standing on it is safe, and that the only number anyone remembers about the place is the one that was never able to fall.
— Nadia Berthold
